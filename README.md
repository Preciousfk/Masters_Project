# MSc Data Science Dissertation
This dissertation investigates implicit cultural bias in user-curated Afro-centric playlists on Spotify and examines its implications for cross-cultural music representation within African music ecosystems. While existing research has largely focused on algorithmic and editorial curation, limited attention has been given to how user-generated playlists may reproduce or amplify structural inequalities in cultural visibility. Using data collected from Spotify’s Web API, this study analyses 52 user-curated Afro-centric playlists alongside 5 algorithmically curated playlists, encompassing over 5,900 tracks, 2,200 albums, 332 genres, and 171 artist nationalities.
A multi-method quantitative framework is employed to assess cultural representation across three dimensions: nationality, genre, and artist visibility. Concentration Ratios (CR₁₀) and Gini coefficients are used to quantify dominance and inequality, while chi-square tests of homogeneity and independence evaluate structural differences within and between playlist types. Jensen–Shannon Divergence is applied to measure distributional divergence between algorithmic and user-curated playlists.
The results reveal extreme concentration and inequality in nationality representation, with a small number of African countries dominating exposure across playlists. Genre representation exhibits strong homogenisation around globally popular styles, while artist exposure reflects pronounced superstar effects. Comparative analysis shows that algorithmic and user-curated playlists share similar dominant nationalities and genres, but algorithmic playlists exhibit substantially higher concentration at the artist level. Overall, the findings demonstrate that user-curated Afro-centric playlists do not mitigate representational bias; instead, they reinforce existing popularity structures and visibility imbalances. This study contributes empirical evidence to debates on cultural diversity, algorithmic influence, and equity in digital music platforms.
